Computational materials science

From microscopic mechanisms to materials design

Y-Matter Lab studies how atomic structure, electronic states, magnetism, lattice dynamics, defects, and interfaces give rise to useful material properties. We combine first-principles calculations, atomistic simulations, and machine learning to understand existing materials and identify promising new candidates.

We are especially interested in

  • Information materials with ferroelectric, ferromagnetic, or multiferroic properties.
  • Energy materials for energy conversion, energy recovery and storage.
  • Quantum materials with intriguing quantum phenomena espeically superconductivity and magnetism.
  • Computational workflows that connect theory, data, and experiment.

01

Quantum Materials

We study superconductors, hydrides, two-dimensional systems, polar metals, magnetic materials, and oxide interfaces where electronic, structural, magnetic, and vibrational degrees of freedom are strongly coupled.

  • Electron-phonon coupling, anharmonicity, and superconductivity.
  • Pressure, strain, dimensionality, and chemical tuning of quantum phases.
  • Microscopic mechanisms behind emergent electronic and magnetic responses.

Key questions

  • Can conventional superconductors reach high Tc without extreme pressure, and which chemistries make this possible?
  • How does quantum nuclear motion change stability and pairing in light-element compounds?
  • Which new electronic phases appear at oxide interfaces, or when polarity coexists with metallicity?

Hydride superconductors at ambient pressure

A central theme is conventional, phonon-mediated superconductivity in hydrides at or near ambient pressure. With collaborators, we combine machine learning with high-throughput ab initio calculations to map large materials spaces: one search of more than 150 000 compounds found about 50 hydrides with predicted Tc above 20 K. Screening the thermodynamically stable cubic hydrides of the GNoME database, we identified 25 superconductors with Tc above 4.2 K, the highest being 17 K in LiZrH6Ru. We also test claims critically: our crystal structure searches for the Lu–N–H system find no stable phase with high-temperature superconductivity near ambient pressure.

Quantum anharmonic effects

High-throughput screens usually work in the harmonic approximation. Because hydrogen is so light, ionic quantum and anharmonic effects can change both dynamical stability and Tc. Including them, we predict that RbPH3 is thermodynamically stable at about 30 GPa in a perovskite structure and stays dynamically stable down to ambient pressure, with a predicted Tc around 100 K. We treat these effects with the stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation (SSCHA), accelerated by machine-learned interatomic potentials, for example in LaBeH8 and metal-stuffed B–C clathrates, and we are building crystal structure prediction schemes that include anharmonic lattice dynamics.

Polar metals and oxide interfaces

We design polar metals such as BiPbTi2O6, in which polar displacements coexist with metallic conduction and can be switched through interfacial coupling to a ferroelectric. We also study emergent magnetism and Berry-curvature effects at oxide interfaces.

02

Information Materials

We investigate ferroelectric, ferromagnetic, piezoelectric, and multiferroic materials for electronics and spintronics. Much of this work focuses on how polarization, magnetism, defects, and interfaces can be controlled.

  • Ferroelectric switching, tunnel junctions, and photovoltaic response.
  • Multiferroics, magnetoelectric coupling, and magnetic anisotropy.
  • Complex oxide thin films, heterostructures, vacancies, and interfacial reconstruction.

Key questions

  • How thin can a ferroelectric become and still switch reliably?
  • How do defects, chemical substitution, and interfaces control polarization, magnetism, and their coupling?
  • Which material designs lead to multi-level, low-power memory and optoelectronic devices?

Atomic-scale ferroelectrics and tunnel junctions

Much of this work is done with experimental groups who grow and measure thin films, while we build first-principles models of structure, polarization, and electronic states. In Sm-substituted layered bismuth oxide, a ferroelectric hysteresis loop is observed down to about 1 nm, and first-principles calculations point to a lone-pair-driven ferroelectric. Tunnel junctions built on this material keep a tunnelling electroresistance of 7 × 105 at 1 nm thickness and support up to 32 resistance states. Our DFT+U calculations of the interface show how switching the polarization turns the interfacial layers of the Nb:SrTiO3 electrode between metallic and insulating, which explains the large resistance contrast.

Chemical design of ferroic films

Inequivalent cation substitution, oxygen vacancies, and anion (sulfur) substitution are used to tune polarization, band gap, and magnetic order. Examples include enhanced ferroelectric photovoltaic responses in double-perovskite films, robust ferroelectricity through polarization rotation in sulfurized BiFeO3, and stronger magnetic ordering in super-tetragonal BiFeO3.

Theory-led device concepts

On the theory side, we have proposed a monolayer two-dimensional ferroelectric tunnel junction based on doped SnSe, and predicted robust ferroelectricity in the nitride perovskite LaWN3.

Energy Materials

We use theory and simulation to explore materials for thermoelectric conversion, catalysis, energy storage, and energy harvesting. A central theme is the relation between local structure, lattice dynamics, transport, and functional response.

  • Thermoelectrics with low thermal conductivity and favorable electronic structure.
  • Negative thermal expansion and thermal transport controlled by phonons.
  • Polar, defective, surface, and interface effects in energy-related processes.

Key questions

  • Which bonding, band-structure, and phonon features give low thermal conductivity and high thermoelectric efficiency?
  • Which local structures and lattice dynamics drive negative or near-zero thermal expansion, and can it be combined with low thermal conductivity?
  • How can polarization and surfaces be used to boost catalytic activity?

Thermal transport and thermoelectrics

We connect chemical bonding and lattice dynamics with heat flow. In the PbSe/PbTe monolayer heterostructure, weak, polar-covalent Pb–Se and Pb–Te bonds with occupied antibonding states lead to ultralow lattice thermal conductivity, which we compute including three- and four-phonon scattering. With experimental groups, we have also studied band engineering in bulk thermoelectrics, such as the alignment of nested conduction bands in Pb7Bi4Se13 and triple doping in Cu2SnSe3.

Anomalous thermal expansion

In Cd2Re2O7-based oxides, synchrotron measurements and phonon calculations together show that soft acoustic phonons with large negative Grüneisen parameters, along with locally ordered Re–Re dimers, give both negative or zero thermal expansion and ultralow thermal conductivity (0.38–0.56 W m−1 K−1). In PbTiO3-based ferroelectrics, we have studied how mixed anions such as sulfur enhance negative thermal expansion.

Polarization-enhanced catalysis

Our DFT+U calculations show that hydroxyl groups adsorbed on BiFeO3 reverse its surface polarization and lower the free-energy cost of hydrogen and oxygen evolution. The computed OER overpotential drops from 0.81 to 0.64 V, consistent with the lower overpotential measured after electrolysis on BiFeO3/nickel-foam electrodes.

Methods

How We Work

First-Principles and Atomistic Simulations

Density functional theory, phonon calculations, lattice dynamics, molecular dynamics, and finite-temperature simulations connect composition and structure with stability, transport, phase transitions, and electron-phonon physics.

Materials Informatics

Machine learning and data-driven workflows help us search large materials spaces, build structure-property models, and prioritize candidates for detailed study, especially when available data are limited.

Theory-Experiment Integration

Computation is used to interpret measurements, test mechanisms, and suggest new compositions, strain states, defects, interfaces, and growth conditions for experimental collaborators.

Methods & Tools

Codes, Workflows, and Computing

The tools we use most often, grouped by task. Tools tagged we contribute are codes and methods our group has helped develop.

Ab initio codes

Density functional theory with VASP and Quantum ESPRESSO, plus WIEN2k and Elk for all-electron calculations. Phonons and thermal transport with phonopy and phono3py. Electron–phonon superconductivity with EPW, Migdal–Eliashberg theory, and superconducting DFT. Quantum transport with DFT plus non-equilibrium Green’s functions (QuantumATK), and chemical bonding with LOBSTER.

  • SSCHA we contribute The stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation, for ionic quantum and anharmonic effects on structure, phonons, and superconductivity.
  • OSEP we contribute The orbital selective external potential method, which shifts selected orbitals to switch off specific hybridizations and identify the mechanism behind a property, such as lone-pair-driven ferroelectricity or magnetic anisotropy (review, lone pairs, magnetic anisotropy).

Crystal structure prediction

Global structure searches with CALYPSO, CrySPY, USPEX, MAGUS, minima hopping, and genetic algorithms, used to find ground states of new compounds, phases under pressure, and substituted or defective structures.

  • Anharmonic structure prediction Iterative-learning searches that rank candidates by their SSCHA free energy, so that quantum and anharmonic effects enter the phase diagram (Phys. Rev. B 2026).

Computational workflow

High-throughput workflows that screen databases of 105–106 compounds, built on pymatgen and ASE, and run on supercomputers including the EuroHPC system LUMI and the DIPC Supercomputing Center.

  • QuAcc we contribute The Quantum Accelerator, a workflow library for high-throughput atomistic simulations (paper).
  • sumo we contribute A toolkit for analysing and plotting results of ab initio calculations, such as band structures, densities of states, and phonons.

Machine learning

We use machine learning both to predict properties and to design materials, and we train interatomic potentials that make expensive simulations affordable.

  • Forward prediction Models that map composition and structure to properties for fast screening, such as graph neural networks (ALIGNN) that predict electron–phonon coupling and Tc across large materials databases.
  • Inverse design Working backwards from a target property to candidate compositions and structures, which are then checked with ab initio calculations and, with collaborators, in experiment.
  • Training MLIPs Machine-learning interatomic potentials trained on DFT data, from moment tensor potentials to fine-tuned atomistic foundation models such as MatterSim, used for SSCHA calculations, molecular dynamics, and large structure searches.
